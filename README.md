# Australia-Plastic-Recycling-Gap-Policy-Research-Report

A qualitative policy research study on why Australia is missing its national plastic recycling targets, and what a better-performing system would look like.

This was a group project (group of three) for **BMO7006 Applied Business Project** at Victoria University. It uses secondary data only, such as government reports, parliamentary inquiries, industry data and international policy documents.

## Summary

Australia's plastic recycling rate is about **13%**, far below the national target of **70% of plastic packaging recycled by 2025**. The study finds that the gap is a **governance design problem**, not a technical or consumer problem.

## Research questions

| # | Question |
| --- | --- |
| RQ1 | What progress has Australia made toward its national plastic recycling targets under the Recycling and Waste Reduction Act 2020 and National Plastics Plan? |
| RQ2 | What are the key barriers preventing Australia from closing the gap between its targets and actual recycling outcomes? |
| RQ3 | How does Australia's plastic waste policy approach compare with policy models in other circular-economy-leading jurisdictions? |

## Key findings

- **Demand is the bottleneck, not capacity.** Only about 50% of processing capacity is being used because demand for recycled material is weak. Around 84% of plastic still ends up in landfill.
- **Four reinforcing barriers** keep the gap in place:
  1. Fragmented, poorly enforced regulation across nine jurisdictions
  2. Recycled plastic cannot compete with virgin plastic on cost
  3. Collection has grown faster than the capacity to process what is collected
  4. Consumers are willing to recycle correctly but are held back by system complexity and inconsistent labelling
- **The 2022 REDcycle collapse** (Australia's largest soft-plastic collection scheme) exposed all four barriers at once.
- **Australian producers pay only about 10%** of end-of-life packaging costs. Councils and taxpayers pay the rest, which reduces the incentive to invest in recyclable design.
- **Leading jurisdictions share three features Australia lacks:** mandatory producer financial responsibility, binding recycled content targets, and design-for-recyclability standards. The EU is the main comparison.
- **There is no single EPR model.** Across 25 EU member states with EPR systems, design choices vary widely, so Australia needs a version that fits its federal structure.

## Recommendations

1. **Legislate a mandatory, nationally consistent Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) scheme**, with binding timelines and penalties, minimum recycled content for packaging, and government procurement that favours recycled content
2. **Replace voluntary targets with binding obligations** across all states and territories, including standardised disposal instructions and producer take-back (the 80% labelling target had no enforcement and awareness rose only 2 to 3 percentage points)
3. **Adopt a hybrid EPR model** drawn from EU design features, rather than copying any single country

## Method

- **Design:** Qualitative secondary analysis. The unit's rules did not allow primary fieldwork
- **Data:** About 15 sources across the three research questions, including the National Plastics Plan 2021, DCCEEW plastics flow data, APCO reporting, CSIRO and ACOR reports, the Senate inquiry "Drowning in Waste", the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, Eurostat and the OECD Global Plastics Outlook
- **Analysis:** Thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's six-phase approach, with manual coding and a traceable excerpt sheet of quotes and sources
- **Reliability:** Themes were cross-checked across sources. For RQ2, consumer survey results were checked against an independent bin audit, and cases that did not fit a theme were kept in the report instead of being dropped
